# Does a moving viewpoint change the exoplanet sky?

**Biswajit Jana | Independent discussion note | 13 October 2026**

[Read the two-page PDF](Moving_Observer_Exoplanet_Sky_Community_Discussion.pdf) · [Finding Earth 2.0: Discovery Universe](https://biswajit1999.github.io/finding-earth-2/universe/) · [Galactic context](https://biswajit1999.github.io/finding-earth-2/galaxy/) · [Related LinkedIn post](https://lnkd.in/p/eGisSr_x)

## The question

While exploring the sky map in **Finding Earth 2.0**, I wondered what happens to the view as the Solar System and exoplanet host stars follow their respective trajectories around the Galaxy.

Suppose the Sun moves from **A** to **B** and eventually **C**. Other stars are moving too, at different velocities and in different directions. Will the relative geometry change which planetary systems we can see, especially through transit photometry? Can it lead to both gains and losses in detectability? On what timescale does this become scientifically important, rather than just a theoretically possible geometric change?

This is an open question I would be glad to hear experts' views on, particularly any important dynamical or observational considerations I may be missing. **The PDF is a discussion prompt, not an observational finding or a claimed planet discovery.**

## Where the numbers come from

The versioned **23 September 2026** Finding Earth 2.0 archive snapshot contains **6,366 confirmed planet records**, of which **6,338 have usable distances and are plotted** (representing **4,747 unique host stars**); 28 planet records have no usable host distance for this view. A plotted planet is located at its *host-star* coordinates; multiple planets sharing a host are not multiple independently moving stars.

Using a nominal Sun--Galactic-centre circular speed of **233 km/s**, the illustrative path length is
```text
100 years:     ~7.35 x 10^11 km = 0.078 light-years
10,000 years:  ~7.35 x 10^13 km = 7.8 light-years
100,000 years: ~7.35 x 10^14 km = 78 light-years
```
These values represent **distance travelled along an assumed Galactic orbit**, *not* net displacement from nearby stars. For a host 100 light-years away and relative transverse speed 30 km/s, the small-angle line-of-sight change over 100 years is about **20.6 arcseconds**. This does not mean that an Earth-like planet would necessarily start transiting in 100 years; transit visibility also depends on orbital inclination and orientation.

## Questions for experts

1. What is the most useful way to think about the A-to-B-to-C moving-observer geometry when both observer and host stars move?
2. Are near-boundary transit configurations the clearest example of changes in detectability, or could other techniques be comparably sensitive?
3. What timescales and typical relative velocities make the effect important, and where does straight-line motion stop being a useful approximation?
4. How should we distinguish the orbital motion of stars from the spiral-arm pattern and from changes in local stellar density?
5. Is there a fundamental geometrical simplification or published treatment of this question that I am overlooking?

Any informed comments, corrections, or relevant references would be appreciated.
