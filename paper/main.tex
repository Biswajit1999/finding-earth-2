    \begin{abstract}
    \noindent
    We present a reproducible computational pipeline that searches the
    public astronomical archives for potentially Earth-like exoplanets and
    ranks them on physically motivated, interpretable criteria rather than a
    supervised machine-learning model, for which no labelled training set of
    inhabited exoplanets exists. The pipeline ingests 164{,}209
    provenance-tracked records from twelve NASA Exoplanet Archive tables
    plus an exact-identifier crossmatch against Gaia DR3 astrometry,
    cross-references public radial-velocity time series from the Data \&
    Analysis Center for Exoplanets (DACE) and light curves from the
    Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes (MAST), and propagates every
    published measurement uncertainty through a 4{,}000-draw Monte Carlo per
    planet. Candidates are scored on four independent axes -- Earth
    similarity, conservative habitable-zone consistency, observational
    confidence, and atmospheric-characterisation potential -- combined by a
    non-compensatory weighted geometric mean so that strong performance on
    one axis cannot mask a disqualifying weakness on another. Of 6{,}354
    confirmed planets analysed, only 15 are simultaneously consistent with
    the conservative habitable zone and small enough to be plausibly rocky,
    and only one of those has a dynamically measured mass; the rest carry
    masses predicted from their radius by a mass--radius relation. The
    computed ranking independently recovers Proxima Centauri~b and several
    nearby M-dwarf terrestrial candidates as the leading systems, without
    these being selected by hand; TRAPPIST-1's planets, despite measured
    masses and transit data, are down-weighted because their host's
    effective temperature sits outside the habitable-zone model's calibrated
    range for all but a small fraction of its uncertainty posterior
    (Section~\ref{sec:hz}). We report the
    methodology, the resulting candidate list with full provenance, and the
    limitations of the approach -- most importantly, that the Earth
    Similarity Index computed from data that exists for real exoplanets
    cannot distinguish an Earth-like world from a Venus-like one. No claim
    of habitability confirmation, biosignature detection, or evidence for
    life is made for any object in this catalogue.
    \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

Public reporting on exoplanet discoveries routinely collapses several
distinct claims into a single headline: that a planet is Earth-sized, that it
orbits within its star's habitable zone, that it is ``potentially
habitable,'' and occasionally that it ``could host life.'' These are not
interchangeable statements, and they are supported by different kinds of
evidence. A planet can be Earth-sized and molten; a planet can sit within the
habitable zone and be a volatile-rich mini-Neptune with no solid surface at
all. This project treats that conflation as an engineering problem: every
quantity we compute answers exactly one of these questions, and every result
we report states which question it answers.

We distinguish five properties that are frequently, and incorrectly, treated
as synonyms:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Earth similarity} -- do the bulk radius, density, escape
    velocity, and equilibrium temperature resemble Earth's?
  \item \textbf{Habitable-zone position} -- is the incident stellar flux
    compatible with surface liquid water under a stated climate model?
  \item \textbf{Rocky plausibility} -- does the radius fall below the regime
    in which planets are predominantly volatile-rich?
  \item \textbf{Atmospheric observability} -- could an atmosphere be
    characterised with current instrumentation?
  \item \textbf{Evidence for life} -- not established for any object in
    this work, and not estimated by any metric we compute.
\end{itemize}

Sections~\ref{sec:data}--\ref{sec:methods} describe the data sources and
methodology; Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the resulting population
statistics and candidate ranking; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses
observational selection effects and the biosignature-interpretation
literature; Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states what this analysis cannot
establish; Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes.

\section{Data and Provenance}
\label{sec:data}
% =============================================================================

The analysis spine is the NASA Exoplanet Archive's Planetary Systems
Composite Parameters table \citep{NasaExoplanetArchive}, cross-checked
against the per-publication Planetary Systems table
\citep{NasaExoplanetArchivePS} to count independent references and measure
inter-publication disagreement. Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists every
archive table ingested and the number of rows retrieved in the analysis run
reported here. Every retrieval is
recorded in a manifest carrying the literal query string, the resolved URL,
a UTC timestamp, and a SHA-256 hash of the payload as received, so that
archive drift (a published value changing between two dates) is detectable
rather than silent. Raw payloads are not redistributed; they are
reconstructible from the recorded query against the live archive.

Public radial-velocity time series were retrieved from DACE
\citep{Dace2015}, including the stellar activity indicators (log
$R'_{\mathrm{HK}}$, H$\alpha$, the Ca~\textsc{ii} H\&K index, and the
cross-correlation-function bisector span) measured from the same spectra.
Public light curves were retrieved from MAST via metadata-first search.
Entity resolution across survey identifiers (Gaia, TIC, EPIC, K2, TOI) used
the archive's own alias-lookup service rather than string similarity, which
we consider an unreliable basis for joining astronomical catalogues.

Separately, every confirmed-planet host star was cross-matched against Gaia
DR3 \citep{GaiaDR3} by its exact \texttt{source\_id} -- extracted from the
\texttt{gaia\_dr3\_id} string the NASA archive already records for 94\% of
confirmed planets (4,408 of 4,764 host systems) -- rather than by sky
position, so the match carries no tolerance radius or ambiguity to flag. This
adds an astrometric distance independent of the archive's adopted
\texttt{sy\_dist} (\texttt{1000\,/\,parallax}, agreeing with the archive to a
median of 0.7\% across all matched hosts; Figure~\ref{fig:gaia}) and the
Renormalised Unit Weight Error (RUWE), Gaia's own single-star astrometric
fit-quality statistic: 412 planets' hosts exceed the conventional RUWE > 1.4
threshold associated with a poorly-fit or unresolved-binary solution, context
relevant to any candidate whose stellar (and hence planetary) radius rests on
an assumption of single-star astrometry. Gaia's own derived astrophysical
parameters are not blended into the adopted stellar parameters used
elsewhere in this pipeline, to avoid reintroducing the single-paper-per-row
inconsistency Section~\ref{sec:data} already notes for the composite table.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Mass provenance}
\label{sec:massprov}

The archive's composite mass column mixes four categories of evidence that
must not be treated as equivalent: a directly measured dynamical mass; a
radial-velocity minimum mass $M\sin i$; a mass predicted from the radius via
a mass--radius relation; and a value reported only as an upper limit. Of
6{,}354 confirmed planets, 2{,}240 (35\%) have a measured mass, 890 (14\%)
carry an $M\sin i$ lower limit, 218 (3\%) carry an upper limit only, and
2{,}975 (47\%) carry a mass inferred from the radius. Using an inferred mass
to compute bulk density or escape velocity re-encodes the radius rather than
adding independent information, which would make a similarity index appear
to combine four independent properties while in fact being driven by one.
We classify every planet's mass provenance explicitly and discount inferred
masses in the observational-confidence score (Section~\ref{sec:confidence}).

\subsection{Habitable-zone model}
\label{sec:hz}

Habitable-zone boundaries follow \citet{Kopparapu2013}, using the corrected
coefficients published in the erratum \citep{Kopparapu2013Erratum} rather
than the original Table~3, which the arXiv preprint of the paper still
carries and which differs from the corrected value by over a percent in
flux at the inner edge. The effective stellar flux at a boundary is
\begin{equation}
  \Seff = \Seff_{,\odot} + aT + bT^2 + cT^3 + dT^4,
  \label{eq:seff}
\end{equation}
with $T = \Teff - 5780\,\mathrm{K}$, valid for $2600\,\mathrm{K} \le \Teff
\le 7200\,\mathrm{K}$; outside this range the model returns an explicit
``not applicable'' value rather than a silently extrapolated one. Five
boundaries are computed from Equation~\eqref{eq:seff} with the coefficients
of \citet{Kopparapu2013Erratum} (recent Venus, runaway greenhouse, moist
greenhouse, maximum greenhouse, early Mars), and the corresponding orbital
distance is $d = \sqrt{L/\Seff}$~au, with $L$ in solar luminosities. We report two zone definitions separately: the
\emph{conservative} zone (runaway greenhouse to maximum greenhouse) and the
\emph{optimistic} zone (recent Venus to early Mars), because the
disagreement between them reflects genuine unresolved climate-modelling
uncertainty rather than noise to be averaged away.

TRAPPIST-1's host star ($\Teff = 2566\,\mathrm{K}$) sits 34~K below the
model's stated validity floor. We report its planets' habitable-zone status
both strictly (undetermined) and under an explicitly flagged extrapolation
that clamps the temperature to the boundary, and we report what fraction of
the Monte Carlo posterior actually falls within the valid range before
quoting a clamped probability.

\subsection{Earth Similarity Index}
\label{sec:esi}

We compute the Earth Similarity Index of \citet{SchulzeMakuch2011}. Writing
$F_i = 1 - \left|\frac{x_i - x_{i,0}}{x_i + x_{i,0}}\right|$ for each of the
four properties (radius, weight $w=0.57$; density, $w=1.07$; escape
velocity, $w=0.70$; temperature, $w=5.58$), the global index is the
four-variable geometric mean
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ESI}_{\mathrm{global}} = \prod_{i=1}^{4} F_i^{\,w_i/4}.
  \label{eq:esi}
\end{equation}
We compute this in two tiers for reporting purposes -- an interior tier
(radius, density) and a surface tier (escape velocity, temperature) -- each
formed as the \emph{product} of its two already-weighted terms
($F_i^{w_i/2}$, i.e. $n=2$ within a two-property tier), with the two tiers
then combined by a single geometric mean
($\mathrm{ESI}_{\mathrm{global}} = \sqrt{\mathrm{ESI}_{\mathrm{interior}}
\cdot \mathrm{ESI}_{\mathrm{surface}}}$) so that $\mathrm{ESI}_{\mathrm{interior}}$
and $\mathrm{ESI}_{\mathrm{surface}}$ remain individually inspectable. This is
algebraically identical to Equation~\eqref{eq:esi}; an earlier implementation
took an additional, incorrect square root when forming each tier, which
silently compounded to $F_i^{w_i/8}$ overall and systematically pushed every
non-Earth score toward 1. We verify the vectorised implementation against an
independently coded flat evaluation of Equation~\eqref{eq:esi} in
\texttt{tests/test\_habitability\_esi.py} for the Solar System controls and
200 random parameter draws.

The published index references its temperature term to Earth's
\emph{surface} temperature (288~K). Exoplanet catalogues supply only
\emph{equilibrium} temperature, which excludes greenhouse warming by
construction. We therefore reference the temperature term to Earth's own
equilibrium temperature (254~K, computed at the same Bond albedo used
throughout this pipeline) rather than mixing the two conventions. The
consequence must be stated plainly: Venus scores $\mathrm{ESI} = 0.92$ under
this definition, because its high Bond albedo gives it an equilibrium
temperature \emph{cooler} than Earth's. An Earth Similarity Index computed
from data that actually exists for real exoplanets cannot separate a
temperate rocky world from a runaway-greenhouse one. We verify this is not
an implementation artefact by running Venus through the identical pipeline
as a labelled control (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Uncertainty propagation}
\label{sec:mc}

Every parameter with a published asymmetric uncertainty is sampled from a
two-piece (split) normal distribution: draws with $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$
are scaled by the upper reported uncertainty when $z>0$ and the lower
uncertainty when $z \le 0$. We use 4{,}000 draws per planet with a fixed
seed. Draws that are non-positive on a positive-definite quantity (radius,
mass, temperature) are rejected and redrawn rather than clipped to zero,
which would bias the posterior median upward for poorly constrained small
planets. Parameters with a value but no published uncertainty are sampled
as a delta function, and the resulting artificially narrow posterior is
tracked explicitly (\texttt{mc\_uncertainty\_coverage}) rather than treated
as equivalent to a genuinely well-measured quantity.

\subsection{Rocky plausibility}

Following \citet{Rogers2015} and the radius-valley result of
\citet{Fulton2017}, rocky plausibility is modelled as a logistic function
of radius,
\begin{equation}
  p(\mathrm{rocky}) = \left[1 + \exp\!\left(\frac{\Rearth - 1.6}{0.20}\right)\right]^{-1},
\end{equation}
centred on $1.6\,\Rearth$ and spanning the observed $1.5$--$2.0\,\Rearth$
radius valley, rather than imposing a hard cutoff the data does not
support.

\subsection{Composite ranking}
\label{sec:confidence}

Four component scores are computed for every planet: Earth similarity (the
Monte Carlo median ESI), conservative habitability (habitable-zone membership
probability, multiplied by the fraction of Monte Carlo draws for which the
host's sampled effective temperature actually fell inside the habitable-zone
model's calibrated range, multiplied by rocky plausibility -- a product, so
neither term alone can rescue a disqualified planet, and a host whose Teff
sits mostly outside the model's domain cannot read as a confident member on
the strength of the small minority of draws the model could evaluate),
observational
confidence (a weighted combination of mass-provenance quality, propagated
uncertainty coverage, independent reference depth, and inter-publication
agreement), and characterisation potential (the Transmission Spectroscopy
Metric of \citealt{Kempton2018}, undefined for non-transiting planets). The
four are combined by a weighted \emph{geometric} mean,
\begin{equation}
  I = \exp\!\left(\sum_i w_i \ln \max(s_i, \epsilon)\right), \quad
  \sum_i w_i = 1,\ \epsilon = 0.01,
  \label{eq:composite}
\end{equation}
with default weights 0.35 (similarity), 0.40 (habitability), 0.25
(confidence), and 0.0 (characterisation, excluded by default because it
structurally penalises non-transiting planets such as Proxima Centauri~b
for a reason unrelated to habitability). A geometric mean is
non-compensatory: an object with excellent measurements and strong
observability but zero habitable-zone consistency still receives a
near-zero composite index, which an arithmetic mean would not guarantee.

\subsection{Transit and radial-velocity validation}
\label{sec:validation}

For deep-dive systems with public MAST light curves, we detrend with a
Savitzky--Golay filter whose window is forced to at least three times the
transit duration, clip outliers upward only (a symmetric clip would remove
the transit itself), fold on the published ephemeris after converting it
into the mission's own time system, and fit a trapezoid. Every fit is
checked against the published depth and rejected as unvalidated beyond a
factor of 1.6 disagreement; validated on four bright benchmark planets
(HD~189733~b, WASP-39~b, HD~209458~b, WASP-19~b) at 0.77--0.99 of the
published depth.

For radial-velocity analysis, every candidate period is checked against the
periodograms of the co-measured stellar activity indicators, because a
rotating, spotted star produces an apparent velocity signal with no planet
behind it. A circular-orbit fit is reported as a measurement only if it
passes three criteria: at least 20 velocities, amplitude significance
$\ge 3\sigma$, and residual scatter below five times the fitted amplitude.
This gate was added after an unconstrained fit to 33 velocities of
TRAPPIST-1 returned an apparent 10--41~$\Mearth$ planet for objects whose
true masses are near one Earth mass.

composite Earth-2.0 index (Equation~\ref{eq:composite}), computed with the
default weights and never hand-selected. The ranking independently recovers
Proxima Centauri~b, GJ~1061~d, GJ~1002~b/c, Wolf~1069~b, Teegarden's Star~c,

planets the literature already treats among the foremost temperate

implemented correctly rather than as a novel population discovery.

\subsection{Evidence coverage}

A high composite index built on weak evidence is a different result from a
high index built on strong evidence, and a single table column cannot make
that distinction visible. Figure~\ref{fig:evidence} shows, for each of the
22 highest-ranked candidates, the actual evidential basis behind its score:
mass provenance quality, propagated-uncertainty coverage, independent
reference depth, habitable-zone model validity, and the availability of
transiting, radial-velocity, and spectroscopic data. The pattern is
immediate: the RV-detected M-dwarf candidates (Proxima Centauri~b,
GJ~1002~b/c, Teegarden's Star~c, Wolf~1069~b, GJ~667~C~f) score strongly on
similarity and habitability but carry $M\sin i$ masses and no transit or
spectroscopic data, while the transiting Kepler/TOI candidates
(Kepler-1649~c, Kepler-1229~b, TOI-700~d and others further down the list)
have masses predicted from radius rather than measured independently. Only
two systems in the top 22 combine a dynamically measured mass with either
population -- GJ~1061~d (non-transiting) and LHS~1140~b (transiting) -- which
is itself the evidence-coverage gap this figure exists to expose.

\subsection{Atmospheric spectroscopy}

The archive holds 5{,}948 genuine transmission-spectroscopy measurements
across 104 planets, harmonised here from two incompatible archive depth
representations (percentage transit depth and planet--star radius ratio)
into a single part-per-million scale; failing to harmonise both silently
truncates a spectrum assembled from only one representation. This is
strictly planetary-atmosphere data, kept separate throughout this project
from the archive's stellar-spectra holdings, which constrain the host star
and underpin radial-velocity work but carry no direct information about a
planet's atmosphere. Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum} shows the best-observed
example in the archive, WASP-39~b, with expected molecular band positions
annotated -- explicitly as band positions, never as detections, which are a
matter for the peer-reviewed analyses cited alongside the data, not for a
dashed line drawn by this pipeline.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Observational selection effects}

The discovery-method distribution of the analysed catalogue reflects
instrument sensitivity, not the true underlying exoplanet population.
Transit surveys favour short periods and large planet--star radius ratios;
radial-velocity surveys favour massive, close-in planets around bright,
quiet stars; direct imaging favours young, wide-separation giants. A
temperate Earth-mass planet around a Sun-like star is disfavoured by every
major detection method simultaneously: its transit probability is low, its
expected radial-velocity semi-amplitude is of order 9~cm~s$^{-1}$ -- below
the demonstrated floor of even the highest-precision current
spectrographs -- and it is too close to its host for direct imaging. The
concentration of high-ranking candidates around M dwarfs
(Figure~\ref{fig:hr}) is therefore partly a statement about which stars are
easiest to search, not solely a statement about where temperate rocky
planets preferentially occur.

\subsection{Biosignature context}

This work computes no probability of biology for any planet, and no metric
in this project is designed to approximate one. There is no calibrated
likelihood function for life on exoplanets: a single confirmed inhabited
world, no confirmed uninhabited control with a directly comparable
atmosphere, and an incomplete theory of abiotic false positives for the
candidate biosignature gases. Molecular oxygen and ozone have documented
abiotic production routes -- water photolysis followed by hydrogen escape,
and CO$_2$ photolysis in a dry atmosphere -- that are expected to be
especially important around M dwarfs, whose extended pre-main-sequence
ultraviolet luminosity can drive several ocean masses of water loss before
the host settles onto the main sequence. Methane has geological sources
(serpentinisation, volcanism) independent of biology. A credible biological
interpretation of any future atmospheric detection would require, at
minimum: simultaneous disequilibrium between multiple species rather than a
single gas; a characterised stellar ultraviolet environment; reported
cloud- and haze-degeneracy constraints; explicit retrieval degeneracies
rather than a single best-fit abundance; and independent reproduction
across instruments or analysis pipelines.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
% =============================================================================

We state four limitations as prominently as the results, because each was
discovered by empirically testing the pipeline against known values rather
than assumed in advance.

\textbf{The Earth Similarity Index cannot separate Earth from Venus.} Venus
scores $\mathrm{ESI}=0.93$ (Section~\ref{sec:esi}) because its high albedo
gives it an equilibrium temperature cooler than Earth's, and equilibrium
temperature is the only temperature exoplanet catalogues can supply. This
is a property of the available observations, not a defect of this
implementation.

\textbf{Roughly half of catalogue masses were never measured.} 2{,}975 of
6{,}354 planets (47\%) carry a mass predicted from the radius
(Section~\ref{sec:massprov}); density and escape velocity derived from such
a mass add no information beyond the radius itself.

\textbf{The habitable-zone model does not formally cover TRAPPIST-1's
host.} At $\Teff = 2566$~K, 34~K below the model's stated floor, its
planets' habitable-zone status is reported both strictly and under an
explicitly flagged extrapolation (Section~\ref{sec:hz}), and the
conservative-habitability score used in ranking is discounted by the
fraction of the Monte Carlo posterior actually inside the model's valid
range (Section~\ref{sec:confidence}) rather than treating the conditional
probability among only the valid draws as if it applied to the whole
posterior -- which is why TRAPPIST-1's planets, prominent in earlier runs of
this pipeline, do not appear in Table~\ref{tab:top10} or among the top 22
candidates in Figure~\ref{fig:evidence}, despite their otherwise strong
Earth-similarity scores.

\textbf{An Earth twin's atmospheric transmission signal is of order
1~part-per-million} for a realistic nitrogen--oxygen atmosphere around a
Sun-like star -- roughly an order of magnitude below a hydrogen-dominated
atmosphere of the same scale height, and below demonstrated JWST precision.
Detecting an Earth analogue and characterising its atmosphere remain
separated by a generation of instrumentation.

Full Gaia DR3 astrometric bulk crossmatching and the ESO Science Archive
were evaluated during the research phase and not integrated into this
release; neither was required to reach a scientifically meaningful sample
size, and Gaia identifiers already reachable through the archive's own
alias service provide entity resolution without a second bulk-archive
integration.

% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% =============================================================================

The search for a second Earth is not currently constrained by how many
exoplanets are known. Of 6{,}354 confirmed planets, only 15 are
simultaneously consistent with the conservative habitable zone and
plausibly rocky, and only one of those has an actually measured mass. The
search is constrained by how few candidates are measured well enough to
support the claim of Earth similarity at all. A physically grounded,
uncertainty-propagating, and evidentially explicit ranking framework
surfaces a short, specific, and reproducible list of candidates worth
prioritising for further observation, while keeping the boundary between
what is measured and what is merely plausible visible at every step rather
than collapsed into a single score.
